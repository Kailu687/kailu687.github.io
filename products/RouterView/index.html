Home network privacy

See what your ISP sees. Then stop it.

RouterView connects to your router, lists every device in your home, and turns cryptic network traffic into plain language: this laptop watched YouTube, that TV phoned an ad server. Then it encrypts the parts your internet provider doesn't need to see, and the sites you visit keep working.

Setup

From unboxed to understood in about five minutes

  1. Find your router

    RouterView spots your gateway on the local network and guesses its maker and model.

  2. Sign in to it

    Enter the router's admin password once. It's stored encrypted on the machine running RouterView, nowhere else.

  3. Confirm the model

    Pick your router from the list so RouterView uses the right connection and knows what it can control.

  4. Meet your devices

    Every phone, laptop, TV and smart plug appears with a name, type and live usage.

  5. Turn on protection

    Choose a level. RouterView shows what will change, applies it, tests it, and undoes it automatically if anything fails.

Protect your internet

Hide what you can, and know what's left

Your ISP carries every packet, so no app can make you invisible to it. What RouterView can do is encrypt the parts that reveal the most, starting with DNS, which spells out each site's name in plain text by default. Typing youtube.com still gets you YouTube.

1 · Private DNS

Beginner

Your router sends site lookups over DNS-over-HTTPS to a private resolver (Quad9 by default, or Cloudflare, Mullvad, AdGuard) instead of your ISP, and drops known trackers.

  • No speed cost you'd notice
  • Every device on the network is covered
  • Hides lookups; site names in connections stay visible

2 · Enhanced Privacy

Recommended

The whole home goes through a WireGuard tunnel to a VPN provider you choose, with DNS inside the tunnel and a kill switch if it drops.

  • Your ISP sees one encrypted stream
  • Exempt devices, like a TV whose streaming app blocks VPNs
  • Your VPN provider now sees what your ISP used to

3 · Strict Privacy

Advanced

Level 2, plus it catches devices that try to go around it: hard-coded DNS gets redirected, bypass routes get blocked, and IPv6 outside the tunnel is switched off.

  • Fails closed: no tunnel, no internet
  • Some smart devices may need an exemption

Addresses still give hints

With Private DNS on, the server addresses you connect to and the site name in each secure connection are still visible. Big shared networks like Google or Cloudflare host thousands of sites behind one address, which blurs this, but a small site on its own server can still be spotted. Levels 2 and 3 close that gap.

About your device list

Your devices sit behind your router, so your ISP normally sees one home address, not each phone's MAC. The exception is a router your ISP supplied and manages remotely (TR-069 or TR-369), which can report connected devices back. RouterView checks for this and tells you if it's switched on.

Nothing breaks quietly

Before any change, RouterView shows what it will do and saves a snapshot of your router's settings. After applying, it checks DNS, IPv4, IPv6, YouTube, Google, WhatsApp and Netflix, and tests for leaks. If one check fails, it restores the snapshot and tells you which one. It only says "protected" when every check passed.

Your data stays home

Device histories and DNS logs live on the machine running RouterView. There's no RouterView account, no cloud copy and no telemetry, and router passwords are encrypted at rest.

Routers

Built first for the router your ISP gave you

Most homes use the router their internet provider installed, so that's where RouterView starts, beginning with the TP-Link Archer routers that providers like ACT Fibernet hand out. Bars show the support level: see devices, see per-device usage, then apply protection on the router itself. Nothing is listed as supported until it has been tested on a real router.

Exact features vary by model and firmware version.

Questions

Before you plug it in

Will RouterView make me invisible to my ISP?

No. Private DNS hides which sites you look up. Enhanced and Strict Privacy also hide which sites and servers you connect to, but your ISP can still see that you use a VPN, and how much data you move and when. Your VPN provider then sees what your ISP used to, so pick one you trust.

Can RouterView read my messages or passwords?

No. Almost everything on the web is HTTPS, so RouterView sees the same kind of outline your router sees: which device, which service, how much data, and when. It never decrypts traffic.

Why does it need my router's admin password?

To read the device list and traffic counters, and to change settings when you turn protection on. The password is encrypted on the machine running RouterView and used only to talk to your router.

Is network activity the same as screen time?

No. A phone can download an update in a drawer, and a laptop can sit on YouTube in a background tab. RouterView shows when a device was active on the network and calls it online activity.

Which routers work today?

Routers running OpenWrt get the full feature set today. Any other router works in basic mode: RouterView finds your devices from the network and shows your ISP exposure report. Full support for ISP-supplied routers comes first, starting with the TP-Link Archer AX23 from ACT Fibernet, which is being tested now. UniFi, MikroTik, ASUS and FRITZ!Box are planned.

What if my ISP gave me the router and locked it down?

That's the case RouterView is built for. Basic mode works on any router today, and RouterView tells you whether your router reports to your ISP. Full support for ISP-supplied TP-Link Archer routers is in testing, and we'll only list it as supported once it works on real hardware.

Can I use it from my phone?

Yes, through the web app. Open it in your phone's browser on home Wi-Fi, enter the pairing code, and add it to your home screen if you like. The agent keeps doing the work.
